**Reflexive Casting** ( sometimes called *cantrip casting* ) is the act of funnelling usable mana, most commonly primed mana, into a spell construct which has been thoroughly memorised in the caster's mind beforehand. Said construct the construct attunes Primed mana where necessary, then applies the resulting Attuned mana to the selected reagent, provided that said reagent satisfies its predefined acceptance conditions if present. If those conditions are not satisfied, execution is inhibited and the supplied mana is either rejected or redirected through a predefined dissipation path.

In sum, reflexive casting is to structured casting what executing a rehearsed procedure is to designing one. As such, reflexive casters do not meaningfully alter a spell construct, but simply activate a spell construct they generally do not fully understand, and which originates from the inherited work of an earlier structured caster, which would have initially designed the spell construct.

Minor variations of commonly found **cantrip constructs** are frequent, and there are sometimes several distinct families of these cantrips with near identical end effect, the foremost of which are the many variants of heating and lighting cantrips, which also happen to be the two most widespread cantrips.

Reflexive casting is the method most novice magi default to using, since it is comparatively simple and generally reliable, as the user only needs minimal understanding of the underlying principles to use it ( notably control of mana and spell construct memorisation ). It is the most widely practised form of magic, and most people at least know a few cantrips by the time they turn 12 seasons, although this fact is most prevalent in cities and larger towns.

## Strengths :

- commonly taught
- reliable after training
- does not require deep theory
- useful for daily cantrips

## Weaknesses :

- limited power
- limited flexibility
- mental fatigue
- construct degradation/interference
- impossible to trivially combine effects

## Limitations :

- **Low parameter range**  
    A person can make a light brighter or dimmer, but cannot redesign the spell into a laser, illusion, or heat lamp.
- **Poor composability**  
    Two cantrips cannot trivially be combined. A person who knows Heating and Launch cantrips would find it impossible to produce a flying fireball without breaking down both cantrips and rebuilding a new one, which enters the domain of Structured casting.
- **Memory burden**  
    Each cantrip occupies trained mental “shape.” Learning too many produces interference, confusion, instability, or reduced reliability. a conventionally trained reflexive caster will generally know no more than two dozen cantrips at a time.
- **Pattern drift**  
    Poorly practised cantrips slowly degrade. This creates folk variants, regional dialects of magic, suboptimal construct forms and, in worse cases, dangerously malformed spells.
- **Safety by overconstraint**  
    Common cantrips are weak not because weak magic is all ordinary people can do, but because public versions are deliberately over-limited to prevent accidents, especially in what is an accepted reagent to target, or with various flow limiting conditions.


NOTES :

It successfully explains:

- widespread everyday magic;
- why ordinary people can cast without theory;
- why safe public spells are deliberately constrained;
- how magical traditions produce regional variants;
- why expertise is not equivalent to memorising more spells.

The “two dozen cantrips” limit is plausible as a worldbuilding rule, but you should decide what exactly occupies memory:

- scaffold geometry;
- component declarations;
- activation sequence;
- sensorimotor mana-control routine;
- all of the above.

Pattern drift is particularly useful. It could produce magical equivalents of:

- accents;
- corrupted copied code;
- folk etymology;
- cargo-cult procedures;
- insecure legacy software;
- incompatible standards.
