# Political Power

Rather than asking only who rules, ask why people obey and what each claimant can actually provide or withhold. Military force, land, food, wealth, religion, administration, historical legitimacy, magical expertise, and access to trained practitioners may all matter at once.

Magic changes political power because reliable practice requires scarce training, instruments, controlled materials, accumulated records, and trusted collaborators. A body that maintains a ward network, vital magical works, a specialist school, or a protected archive can therefore exercise authority without owning every field or household in its vicinity.

Land, labour, and coercion remain important: magical institutions still need food, materials, sites, protection, transport, and people. The aim is not to replace territorial politics with a single magical hierarchy, but to make authority overlap in ways that follow from real dependencies.

## Houses, Guilds, and Higher Authorities

[[Houses|Houses]] may act as professional lineages, landholders, vassals, patrons, local governors, or independent polities according to their resources and bargains. [[Guilds|Guilds]] organise particular trades and professions, sometimes cooperating with Houses and sometimes contesting their control over training, credentials, and valuable knowledge.

Kings, councils, leagues, cities, temples, and empires may claim wider authority. Their size must have consequences: administration, supply, communication, provincial autonomy, and the uneven enforcement of central decisions all become more difficult with distance and diversity.

## Knowledge Networks

Magic requires teachers, apprentices, instruments, and retained practice. The resulting intellectual lineages cross or compete with political borders, and offer the protagonist a route into society. The incentives and failures of this system belong in [[Knowledge Systems|Knowledge Systems]].
